### The four per-hop passages — hop-state details

These are the machinery inventory, delivered at the hop rather than in the
bootstrap. Each ends on what not running the hop strands downstream, which is
the register `/charter`'s "Why `/roadmap` Is Unconditional" already uses. They
sit in the `<!-- details -->` of `hop_brief`, `hop_prd`, `hop_design` and
`hop_plan` respectively, so each is delivered once, on entry to its own hop.

**`hop_brief`:**

> `/brief` converts a topic string into a stated problem and a
> stated outcome, then puts concrete user journeys and a scope
> boundary under them. Two reviewers read the result in parallel:
> one checks that the Problem Statement states a problem rather
> than a smuggled solution and that the journeys are distinct and
> exercisable, the other checks structure and visibility. Both
> must pass before the BRIEF is Accepted. Without this hop, `/prd`
> writes requirements against whatever the topic string implied,
> with no agreed problem to test them for relevance against.

**`hop_prd`:**

> `/prd` turns that problem and outcome into requirements with
> acceptance criteria. Phase 1 is a scoping dialogue with coverage
> tracking, and it hands Phase 2 a set of research leads that
> specialist agents investigate in parallel, so the requirements
> are written against what the codebase and its ecosystem support
> rather than against a guess. A three-agent jury then reads the
> draft, because authors miss ambiguity and testability gaps in
> their own writing. Without this hop, `/design` picks an
> architecture with no statement of what the thing must do:
> nothing bounds the alternatives, and nothing decides when the
> feature is done.

**`hop_design`:**

> `/design` is where the alternatives get weighed. Phase 1
> decomposes the problem into independent decision questions.
> Phase 2 runs `/decision` against each one in parallel and at
> equal depth: a bakeoff with adversarial agents, peer revision
> and cross-examination, per question. Phase 3 cross-validates the
> assumptions those decisions made against each other and always
> runs, even with one decision. Phase 5's security review always
> runs too; its output may be N/A, but the review is not optional.
> Phase 6 checks that the rejected alternatives were argued rather
> than propped up. Without this hop, the PLAN sequences an
> approach nobody compared to anything, and no security review
> happens anywhere in the chain.

**`hop_plan`:**

> `/plan` decomposes the DESIGN into atomic issues, sequences them
> and maps the dependency graph. A value-confirmation guard checks
> that each unit delivers observable incremental value and can
> fail, naming the unit and the reason it failed. Phase 6 hands
> the whole artifact to `/review-plan`, which challenges it on
> scope, design fidelity, whether the acceptance criteria
> discriminate, and sequencing integrity. Design fidelity is the
> check with nothing to read when no DESIGN exists: a PLAN reached
> without one still validates and still passes review, because the
> reviewer can only measure fidelity against what it is given.

The `/brief` passage is the thinnest of the four, because `/brief`'s machinery
genuinely is the lightest. It was not padded; the honest weight is in its last
sentence.

## The Fold State's Details Payload

D1 puts the general-form reduction argument and the four per-type summaries in
`fold`'s `<!-- details -->`, delivered on entry and on re-entry and suppressed
only on a self-loop, which `fold` never takes. The payload below is what goes
there. It is scoped to the pair by construction: it never names a document type
as *the* upstream, because the pair differs per visit and a directive is static
text, and it tells the agent explicitly which two of the four rows are its
question.

> <!-- details -->
>
> You are holding two documents: the one that just landed, and the
> one this hop handed the child as its invocation argument. What
> follows is about those two and about nothing else.
>
> Two documents that restate one problem at two altitudes cost a
> reader two reads for one idea, and an obvious point articulated
> twice reads as ceremony. Sparing the reader that is worth doing,
> and it is the only thing that ever removes a document from a
> `/scope` run. It is worth doing here, about the pair in your
> hands. It is not a reason to want fewer documents in general,
> and it decides nothing about a document nobody has written.
>
> Applying it needs what each of your two documents declares it
> contributes. Each type declares one contribution, quoted here
> from that type's own format reference:
>
> - **BRIEF** — WHY: the problem the feature solves and the
>   outcome a user should experience.
> - **PRD** — WHAT: the requirements the feature must meet and the
>   criteria that decide it is done.
> - **DESIGN** — HOW: the technical approach, the alternatives
>   weighed, and why this one.
> - **PLAN** — WHEN: the order the work happens in, and what each
>   unit depends on.
>
> Find the two rows that describe your edge. The other two are not
> your question. Read the upstream you are holding against its own
> row and ask one thing: does it hold anything beyond that
> contribution which compression into a single section would lose?
>
> If it does, the verdict is `keep`, with a finding naming what
> the upstream holds that the survivor would not. If it does not,
> the verdict is `absorb`, and Phase 2's carry check has to
> confirm every concern landed before anything is deleted.

Three properties of this payload are load-bearing and worth stating in the
DESIGN so a later editor does not break them.

**It contains Appendix A's pinned fragment exactly once.** "Sparing the reader"
appears in the second paragraph and nowhere else in the template, which is what
AC3 counts. Rewriting the sentence to avoid the fragment would satisfy the
prose and fail the check, so the fragment is deliberate.

**The reader-cost sentence is stated about two documents, not three.** The
original said "three documents that restate one problem at three altitudes,"
which is a claim about the chain. Two is a claim about the pair in hand, and it
is the only form R2 permits to be delivered anywhere.

**All four rows appear even though only two apply.** A directive is static and
the edge varies, so the four-row table is the minimum that serves all three fold
visits. The scoping is done by the sentence after the table rather than by
pruning the table, which is why that sentence is not optional.

## Consequences

**Positive.** The pre-hop set stops arguing for an outcome and starts arguing
for the work: the twelve lines of motive that survive all point at running the
chain, and the one passage that pointed the other way is the passage the
incident agent quoted. R2 becomes a property of the file tree plus the graph
rather than a property of prose discipline, since the general form exists in
exactly one place and that place is a state an agent cannot reach without having
walked a hop. The eight `contribution` sites get a referent for the first time,
which was a real defect independent of this work. And an agent that skips a hop
now loses something it can name — `/review-plan` has nothing to read, no
security review runs — rather than losing an abstraction.

**Negative, with what to do about each.**

*The four per-hop passages only reach an agent that arrives at the hop.* That is
the deferral objection the earlier exploration raised against putting the
argument at the hop, and under prose control flow it was decisive. It stops
applying here because the substrate, not the agent's compliance, decides when
the hop state is entered — but it does mean an agent driven past a hop with
`koto next --to` never reads what it strands. The mitigation is R22's typed
entry, not more prose: the bypass is recorded whether or not the argument was
read.

*The file gets 3.6% shorter while the instruction total gets longer.* A
reader expecting the exploration's "meaningfully shorter" will find the number
underwhelming. Say so in the DESIGN rather than let it be discovered: the number
that moved is pre-hop motive, from about 70 lines to about 12.

*The phase-2 twin edit is outside Appendix A's enumeration.* R4 asks for a
disposition on P1 through P6, and this is a seventh change nobody asked for. It
is required by R2(a) read literally, and it is the kind of thing AC6 does not
check. If the DESIGN drops it, R2 is violated in a file the appendix names as a
good example, and the failure survives every acceptance criterion.

*The fold payload lists all four contributions to serve two.* An agent at the
BRIEF-to-PRD edge reads the DESIGN and PLAN rows too. The scoping sentence after
the table is the only thing keeping that from being a per-type inventory
delivered mid-run, and a later editor tidying the table will not know that. It
belongs in a comment beside the payload in the template.

*`## Why Each Hop Is Taken` states no per-type contribution while `fold`'s
details state four.* Two files now describe the same concept at two altitudes,
which is a drift surface. The mitigation is that the template's four rows are
quoted verbatim from the format references, so a check can compare them by
string; the DESIGN should hand that check to D6.
